The Viewer

End-user seeking
content they love

Viewers expected a Netflix-quality experience — regardless of whether the channel had 200 subscribers or 2 million. They didn't care who built it.

  • Frictionless playback — no buffering, no confusion
  • Browse & discover content quickly
  • Download for offline viewing on mobile
  • Cast to TV from phone without thinking about it
  • Captions, audio descriptions, accessible controls
B2C Viewer success metric: retention & completion rate
Core tension

Every design decision had to serve both audiences simultaneously. A creator customization that delights the channel builder can fragment the viewer experience. Finding that balance — without forcing a tradeoff — was the defining design problem of this role.

Explorations — Creator Dashboard IA

Three directions I explored.

Direction A

Unified command center

One dense dashboard surfacing every metric, setting, and action upfront. Maximum information density for power users — all controls always visible.

✕
Rejected Usability tests showed 70%+ of mid-tier creators couldn't find core publish actions. Cognitive overload caused abandonment at the most critical steps.

Direction B

Wizard-style linear flow

Step-by-step guided setup for new creators. Simple, focused, lower friction for first-timers — but forced even experienced creators through the same slow flow every time.

◐
Partially adopted Great for onboarding. Unusable as a daily workflow — power creators were bottlenecked by a flow that assumed every action was new. Kept for setup only.

Direction C  ·  Chosen

Progressive disclosure hub

A clean overview state showing only priority actions and key metrics, with advanced settings surfaced contextually. Fast for experts, safe for beginners.

✓
Chosen direction Reduced time-to-publish by cutting navigation depth. Advanced features available — not hidden, just quiet. Scaled cleanly across creator tiers.

Trade-offs & Pivot

Why I killed
the better-looking
option.

Direction A looked great in a Figma presentation. It failed real users in under 3 minutes. Here's the honest breakdown of what each direction cost us — and what we gained by choosing the harder path.

The Pivot

Engineering estimated Direction C would take 40% longer to build. I made the case to PM using the usability data: the cost of re-engaging churned creators was higher than the sprint cost. We got the time.

Direction A — Rejected
Direction C — Chosen
Creator
onboarding
Direction A

Overwhelming for new creators — 3 of 5 usability participants couldn't find where to upload their first video without prompting.

Direction C

Progressive reveal kept first-time creators on a guided path without locking out experienced ones. Onboarding completion improved measurably.

Power user
workflow
Direction A

Actually strong here — power users wanted everything visible. This was the design's only real strength.

Direction C

Keyboard shortcuts + deep-link support gave power users speed without adding noise for everyone else. Compromise that worked.

Eng.
cost
Direction A

Faster to ship — static layout with fewer contextual rules. This was the main argument for it internally.

Direction C

+40% estimated build time. Justified by retention data showing creator churn correlated directly with dashboard confusion in first 7 days.

Long-term
scalability
Direction A

Adding features would require constant triage of what to de-prioritize on screen. Doesn't scale as the product grows.

Direction C

New features slot into the progressive disclosure model naturally. We added 4 major features over 2 years without a visual redesign.

Honest Retrospective

If I had another 6 months —

↑
I'd have invested earlier in creator onboarding research We discovered the cognitive overload problem late — after Direction A was already scoped. Earlier user testing would have saved a sprint.
↑
I'd have pushed harder on TV accessibility TV app accessibility (screen readers, motor) was under-resourced across the industry. I shipped what was possible, but it wasn't what it should have been.
→
The creator/viewer tension was never fully resolved We managed it well, but "full brand customization" vs. "consistent viewer UX" remained a live argument through my last year. There's no clean answer — only better tradeoffs.
→
Mobile was always underprioritized relative to its usage Roadmap pressure meant TV got resources disproportionate to how creators and viewers actually used the product. Something I'd fight differently with today's data.
